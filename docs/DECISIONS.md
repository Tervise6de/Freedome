# Decisions

The choices worth arguing with, and why they went the way they did.

---

## 1. Generate the scene from code instead of building it by hand

**Decision.** The entire scene is produced by editor scripts. There is no
hand-placed geometry, and re-running the generator reproduces the room exactly.

**Why.** Three reasons, in order of weight:

1. **One source of truth.** Every dimension comes from `ShedDimensions.cs`. The
   scene, the tests and the scale drawings all read it, so they cannot disagree.
   Change the wall height and the room, the assertions and the drawings all
   follow.
2. **Reviewability.** You can read why a stud is where it is. A hand-placed scene
   is a binary blob whose reasoning lives only in the author's memory.
3. **It was the only option available.** This was authored without Unity (see
   KNOWN_ISSUES.md). Hand-placing was not possible. Being honest: constraint came
   first, but the reasoning above is why it would have been a defensible choice
   anyway.

**Cost.** Iterating on a prop means editing code and regenerating rather than
dragging it in the viewport, which is slower for fine art tweaks. Anyone who
takes this forward as an art task will likely want to bake the generated scene
into a hand-editable one at some point.

**Alternative rejected.** ProBuilder, which the asset policy ranks above
procedural meshes. ProBuilder's authoring API is designed for interactive use and
would still have needed a Unity install to drive. It stays in the manifest for
whoever does want to hand-edit.

---

## 2. Chamfered boxes as the default primitive

**Decision.** `MeshBuilder.AddBox` generates a properly chamfered box - six inset
faces, twelve edge chamfers, eight corner triangles - at 44 triangles instead of
12.

**Why.** The single most reliable tell of an unfinished environment is
mathematically sharp edges everywhere. Real edges catch a highlight. A 2 mm
chamfer on every stud, board and trim piece does more for "this is a built
thing" than any texture would.

**Cost.** 3.7x the triangles per box. Mitigated by making the chamfer a
parameter: architecture and hero props pay for it, small background items pass
zero. The chamfer is clamped to a third of the smallest dimension so thin boards
survive, with a test to prove it.

---

## 3. UVs measured in metres

**Decision.** Every face is planar-unwrapped with coordinates in metres, and
every generated texture covers exactly one square metre and tiles seamlessly.

**Why.** Consistent texture scale is an acceptance criterion, and the usual way
to get it wrong is per-object tiling values that drift. With metre UVs there is
nothing to set: a bench top, a wall and a floorboard get identical grain density
because they are all measured the same way. Material tiling stays at 1:1
everywhere.

**Refinement.** Measuring UVs in metres fixes scale but says nothing about
*direction*, and the first implementation picked the axes from each face's
dominant world axis. That put the fibre across every horizontal member: the side
of a top plate faces sideways whichever way the plate runs, so V came out
vertical on plates, rafters, purlins, collar ties, noggins and bench rails.

`MeshBuilder` now builds the frame from a grain direction instead - V follows the
piece's own longest axis, carried through its rotation - which fixes the whole
scene without annotating a single call site. `GrainOverride` handles the cases
where the material has a direction the geometry does not imply. Both behaviours
are pinned by tests, because with no renderer here that is the only way to state
the intent.

---

## 4. Assemblies as single meshes with submeshes

**Decision.** Each logical assembly - a wall, the roof, the workbench,
everything on the shelves - is one `MeshBuilder`, producing one mesh with one
submesh per material and therefore one renderer.

**Why.** Draw calls scale with distinct materials per renderer, not with how many
things there are. The shelf contents are around twenty separate containers and
cost one renderer with six submeshes.

**Cost.** Individual props are not separately selectable, movable or prefabbable.
This is the direct reason `Assets/Game/Prefabs/` is empty. For a static
environment milestone that is the right trade; for a project that later needs to
pick up a bucket, it is not, and those props will need extracting.

---

## 5. Procedurally generated textures rather than authored or downloaded ones

**Decision.** Ten material families, three maps each, generated by code from a
periodic noise library.

**Why.** The asset policy ranks procedural generation third and free licensed
assets fifth, and the strongest possible position on licensing is having nothing
to license. It also means the whole material set regenerates from source with no
binary dependency.

**Cost.** They are placeholder quality and are labelled as such throughout. They
give correct roughness behaviour and correct physical scale - enough to judge
lighting, composition and scale - but they are not scanned or hand-painted
material and should not be shipped as final.

**Alternative rejected.** Free CC0 texture libraries. They would look better
immediately, but downloading was impossible in this environment, and every one
adds a licence row to verify before release.

---

## 6. Physically Based Sky, not an HDRI

**Why.** An HDRI is a third-party binary asset with a licence. The PBR sky is
part of HDRP, needs nothing external, and for a daytime exterior seen only
through a small window and a row of eaves gaps it is more than sufficient.

**Cost.** Less interesting reflections than a good HDRI would give. The two
reflection probes cover what the metal in the room actually needs.

---

## 7. Corrugated sheeting on purlins, no sarking, no ceiling

**Decision.** The roof is bare corrugated steel on purlins over the rafters.

**Why.** It is what a shed of this size and budget has, and it means the player
looks up at real structure - rafters, purlins, collar ties, ridge board - instead
of at a flat plane. It also produces the room's most useful secondary light
source honestly: the corrugation profile does not sit flat on the eaves blocking,
so a row of thin crescents of sky light runs along the top of both long walls.

**Cost.** The light leak is unverified and could read as a seam rather than as
highlights. It is tunable in one place. See LIGHTING.md.

**Alternative rejected.** Sarking boards under the sheeting, which is also
realistic but would seal the eaves and remove the light entirely, leaving the
room with one window and a vent.

---

## 8. Legacy Input Manager, not the Input System package

**Decision.** `Input.GetKey` and `Input.GetAxisRaw`. The Input System package is
deliberately absent from the manifest.

**Why.** The Input System requires a generated actions asset, which is a binary
asset that would have to be hand-authored as YAML in an environment with no
Unity to validate it. Legacy input needs nothing and works out of the box. For
WASD, mouse look and one crouch key, it is entirely adequate.

**Cost.** No rebindable controls, no gamepad, no runtime device switching. If any
of those become requirements, this is the first thing to change.

---

## 9. Pause menu built from code, with legacy uGUI text

**Decision.** The whole menu is constructed in `PauseMenuController.Awake()`
using uGUI and `LegacyRuntime.ttf`.

**Why.** Same reasoning as the scene: no prefab to hand-author blind, no
serialized references to get wrong, and the entire interface is one reviewable
file. TextMeshPro was avoided because it requires importing TMP Essentials
through an editor dialog, which a headless first run cannot do.

**Cost.** Legacy `Text` looks slightly less crisp than TMP. The quality selector
is a cycling button rather than a real dropdown, because a `Dropdown` needs a
template hierarchy that is tedious to build in code. Both are fine for a demo
menu and both are easy to upgrade.

---

## 10. Mono scripting backend, not IL2CPP

**Why.** IL2CPP additionally requires a Visual Studio C++ toolchain on the build
machine. For a project whose build should work anywhere Unity plus the Windows
module is installed, that is a requirement not worth imposing quietly.

**Cost.** Slower managed code and a larger, more reversible build. Neither
matters for an environment demo at 60 fps. Switch to IL2CPP before shipping
anything real.

---

## 11. Mixed lighting, shipped unbaked

**Decision.** Lights are Mixed with shadowmask, the lightmapper is fully
configured, and the scene is committed without a bake.

**Why.** A bake is minutes to hours of CPU time and produces large binary
lightmap assets. Committing them from an environment that cannot verify them
would be committing unverified binaries. The bake is one flag on the build script
(`--bake`).

**Cost.** Anyone opening the project sees a flatter, darker room than intended
and might judge the art direction on it. LIGHTING.md and KNOWN_ISSUES.md both say
so in bold.

---

## 12. Walking speed of 1.5 m/s

**Why.** An unhurried indoor walking pace. Crossing the 6 m length takes about
four seconds. Demo controllers habitually run at 5-6 m/s, which makes any room
feel like a corridor and actively defeats the purpose of a milestone whose whole
point is that the space reads at the right size.

---

## 13. No jump

**Why.** The brief says not to add one unless it is needed to test the
environment, and it is not - there is nothing to jump onto. It is also the single
easiest way for a player to clip through a wall or a roof, and removing it
removes a class of bugs from a milestone whose acceptance criteria include "the
player cannot leave the demonstration environment".

---

## 14. A boundary backstop that is treated as a bug

**Decision.** `PlayAreaBoundary` returns the player to spawn if they end up
outside the interior, logs a warning when it does, and counts the recoveries. A
PlayMode test asserts the count stays at zero.

**Why.** A backstop that quietly rescues the player hides collision gaps forever.
Making it loud and asserting it never fires means it functions as a test, not as
a crutch, while still guaranteeing the acceptance criterion in the shipped build.

---

## 15. Asserting on architectural intent in tests

**Decision.** The EditMode tests assert things like "the door opening is under a
metre wide", "the window sill clears the bench", "the clear aisle is over 2 m",
and "the ridge is under 3.6 m".

**Why.** These look like tautologies over constants, but the constants are
hand-edited and read by a dozen builders. A change that turned the shed into a
6 x 4 room with a 3 m ceiling would generate perfectly happily and would only be
caught by eye. The tests state the intent so that kind of change fails loudly
rather than silently producing a room that is subtly wrong.

---

## 16. Scale drawings generated by parsing the C# source

**Decision.** `Tools/generate_diagrams.py` parses `ShedDimensions.cs` with a
regex and evaluates the constant expressions, rather than restating the numbers.

**Why.** A drawing that restates its numbers is a drawing that will eventually
lie. Parsing the source means the drawings cannot drift.

**Payoff.** This caught three real placement faults - a wheelbarrow through a
wall, a mower overlapping a boot tray, a sawhorse clipping the wheelbarrow - none
of which was visible from reading the code. See KNOWN_ISSUES.md.

**Cost.** The parser handles `public const float` only; the handful of computed
properties are mirrored by hand in the script and flagged in a comment. If that
list grows, the drawings become a drift risk again.


---

## Interaction, and the exception it forces

**Decision.** The shed responds to a use key: the door and the floor panel swing
on hinges, the light switch works, and five loose objects can be picked up and
put down.

**This overturns the original milestone**, which said the environment was to
stay scenery and that the reserved affordances were "architecture only, do not
make them interactive". That was the right call for an environment being
reviewed on how it looks. It stopped being the right call once the question
became whether the room feels like somewhere a person works.

**The line that replaces it.** An interactable does something physical to itself
and nothing else. No flags, no unlocks, no counters, no completion, nothing
required. The moment one object's state changes what another object does, this
is a game and a different set of decisions applies.

**What it costs.** Anything that moves needs its own GameObject, renderer and
collider, which is a direct exception to decision #4 - props are appended into
shared meshes to keep the draw-call count down. The exception is currently eight
objects: the door leaf, the service panel, the switch rocker and five
carryables. Each one is a draw call that used to be free.

Eight was chosen as the point where the room reads as handled without undoing
the batching argument for the other couple of hundred pieces. The window
casement, the vent louvres and the radio stay in their shared meshes and stay
still, which is why three of the six affordances do not move.

**What is not verified.** All of it. Nothing here has run. The hinge offsets and
the carryable placements are pinned by arithmetic in `InteractionTests`, which
caught one placement error - a jar of fixings floating 350 mm in front of the
bench - but arithmetic cannot tell you whether a door opening outward hits the
timber stacked against the wall, or whether a dropped paint tin settles or
jitters.
